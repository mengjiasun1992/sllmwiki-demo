## 3. At a glance

<A small table: what kind of work it is (research paper, position paper, essay, book chapters), its authors, venue and year, its length and the time a full read takes, the question it asks, and its claim in one sentence, each cited.>

## 4. Context

<What the work builds on and pushes against, at the level of a reader from the field: the problem as it stood, the approaches it responds to, and what it adds to them. A few short paragraphs; background the reader already has is named, not taught.>

## 5. The arc

<How the work unfolds: its parts in order, a line or two each, cited to their sections, and how the argument builds from one to the next. A diagram of the argument when it helps.>

## 6. The key part

<The most important idea or result, made clear: what it says, why it holds, and what follows from it, on the work's own example or figure, with a short quotation of its central statement. This section carries the depth: at least 40% of the body.>

## 7. How strong is it

<The evidence the work gives (proofs, experiments, deployments, arguments), the assumptions it rests on, what it leaves out, and where it overreaches, each cited, or marked as this primer's own reading.>

## 8. Should you read it

<Who gains from the full work and what they'd gain beyond this primer; a reading path through its sections (read these, skim these, skip these, cited); then the verdict, as a plain recommendation: read it if…, skip it if….>
